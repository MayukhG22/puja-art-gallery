Madhubani (Mithila Art)

Originating from Bihar, this art was traditionally painted by women on freshly plastered mud walls. It uses vibrant natural dyes and complex geometric patterns to depict mythological epics, nature, and royal court scenes. A defining feature is that no empty space is left on the canvas.

Warli

A tribal art form from Maharashtra, Warli is famous for its extreme simplicity. Using a basic visual vocabulary of circles, triangles, and squares painted in stark white rice paste over an earthy red mud background, it beautifully illustrates the daily life, harvests, and dances of the tribal community.

Pichwai

Originating in Nathdwara, Rajasthan, Pichwai paintings are highly intricate visual stories dedicated to Shrinathji (Lord Krishna). They are characterized by rich, vibrant colors, detailed brushwork, and recurring motifs of lotuses, cows, and peacocks.

Gond

A vibrant tribal art from Madhya Pradesh, Gond paintings are deeply connected to the forest and wildlife. The signature style involves filling shapes with intricate patterns of dots and dashes, creating a sense of movement and life within the animals and trees depicted.

Kalamkari

Hailing from Andhra Pradesh, Kalamkari literally translates to "pen craft." It involves a tedious 23-step process using natural dyes to hand-paint or block-print intricate motifs on cotton textiles, often telling grand stories from the Ramayana and Mahabharata.

Jamini Roy Style

A modern classic from Bengal, Jamini Roy abandoned academic western art to create his own style inspired by the Kalighat Patachitra (scroll paintings). His work is instantly recognizable by sweeping, bold brush strokes, vibrant flat earth colors, and figures with distinctive large, almond-shaped eyes.

Phulkari

Meaning "flower work," Phulkari is the traditional embroidery art of Punjab. It involves weaving bright, colorful silk threads into heavy cotton fabrics (khaddar) to create stunning geometric and floral motifs, often created by women for important life events and celebrations.
